\chapter{Introduction}

\section{The proposal in brief}
The model (M) is the standard soft-margin SVM with Gaussian, polynomial or linear
kernel, used for multi-class classification through the usual One-vs-Rest
construction. We do not modify it.

For (A1) we propose to dualise, not a constraint of the SVM dual, but the
stationarity condition $w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\phi(x_i)$ that the usual derivation uses
to eliminate $w$. Neither constraint of the dual is the difficulty there: relaxing
the equality $y^{\!\top}\alpha=0$ or the box $0\le\alpha\le C$ leaves a quadratic
program with the same dense Hessian $Q$, and in the first case a univariate dual.
The difficulty is $Q$ itself, and $Q$ exists only because $w$ was eliminated. So we
put $w$ back, which turns that identity into a constraint, and relax it.

What is left after the relaxation is a linear program over a box with one equality
constraint, a continuous knapsack that a sort solves in $O(m\log m)$. The dual
function is finite everywhere, since the polytope is compact; it is defined on a
space of dimension $n$ for a linear kernel and $m$ in general; and it is
non-differentiable exactly where the knapsack ties, which is the support-vector
condition $y_if(x_i)=1$. A bundle method is therefore the appropriate algorithm
rather than an ornament.

We also show that the maximum of this dual is the hinge-loss primal, which is how
the SVM appears in the course material on nondifferentiable optimization. We think
the dualisation still earns its place, because it eliminates the bias exactly at
every oracle call, returns the SVM dual variables through the master problem's
multipliers, and brackets the optimal value from both sides. Chapter~\ref{ch:ask}
states the question we would like settled before we begin the experiments.

\section{Description of the task}
This report explores two different optimization algorithms applied to a machine
learning model.

The ML model (M) is a Support Vector Machine-type approach for multi-class
classification, with a choice of kernels, using the standard One-vs-Rest approach.

The first optimization algorithm (A1) is a dual approach applied to the dual
formulation of the SVM. We reintroduce the weight vector $w$ as an explicit
variable, so that the representer identity $w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\phi(x_i)$ becomes a
constraint of the problem, and we dualise \emph{that} identity. The resulting
Lagrangian relaxation is a linear program over a box with a single equality
constraint. This is a continuous knapsack, which a sort solves in $O(m\log m)$. The
dual function is then finite everywhere and non-differentiable, and it lives in a
space of dimension $n$ for a linear kernel, or $m$ in general. We maximise it with a
proximal bundle method. Its master problem is handed to an off-the-shelf QP solver,
which the project statement allows.

The second optimization algorithm (A2) is a general-purpose convex QP solver applied
to the standard SVM dual. No off-the-shelf solver is used elsewhere, save for the
master problem of the bundle method.

Specific discussion is provided in Chapter~\ref{ch:multiclass} about if and how
solving one training problem in the One-vs-Rest approach may provide useful
solutions that help in solving the others.

\section{Scope of this document}
This document covers the formulation only. It states the model, derives the
Lagrangian dual we propose for (A1), describes the bundle method that solves it and
the general-purpose solver used for (A2), and says what we expect each of them to
do. We submit it in advance, as the course guidelines suggest, so that the choice of
what to dualise can be confirmed before the experimental work is done.

Both algorithms have been implemented. The experimental study, with the comparison
between (A1) and (A2), the behaviour of the bundle as the data grows, the
One-vs-Rest transfer, timings and model selection, will form the second part of the
report. No measured result is quoted here.

\chapter{SVM Problem Formulation}
\label{ch:svm}

\paragraph{Notation.} Throughout, $\inner{a}{b}$ denotes the \emph{inner product}
(the scalar product) of two elements of the feature space $\Hs$, a single real
number, $\sum_i a_ib_i$ in the finite-dimensional case. We write it this way rather
than $a^{\!\top}b$ because $\Hs$ may be infinite-dimensional, as it is for the
Gaussian kernel, so there is no finite column of coordinates to transpose. The form
$a^{\!\top}b$ is the special case $\Hs=\R^n$. Ordinary vectors of $\R^m$, such as the multipliers
$\alpha$ and the labels $y$, are written with transposes as usual: $y^{\!\top}\alpha$,
$\alpha^{\!\top}Q\alpha$. Finally $\one$ is the vector of all ones and
$[t]^+=\max\{0,t\}$.

The goal of this chapter is to describe the Support Vector Machine and to derive its
Lagrangian dual, so that the training task becomes a quadratic programming problem.
The material here is standard. We include it because Chapter~\ref{ch:dual} departs
from it at one specific point, the stationarity condition~\eqref{eq:representer},
and that departure is much easier to follow with the usual derivation in front of
the reader.

\section{Soft margin formulation}
Let $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^m$ with $x_i\in\R^n$ and $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, and let
$\phi:\R^n\to\Hs$ be a feature map into a Hilbert space with reproducing kernel
$\kappa(x,x')=\inner{\phi(x)}{\phi(x')}$. We call $\Hs$ the reproducing kernel Hilbert
space (RKHS) of $\kappa$, and it may be infinite-dimensional. Write $\phi_i:=\phi(x_i)$.

A separating hyperplane $H(w,b)=\{x:\inner{w}{\phi(x)}+b=0\}$ classifies correctly
with margin at least one when $y_i(\inner{w}{\phi_i}+b)\ge1$ for all $i$, and the
geometric margin is then $1/\|w\|$, so maximising the margin means minimising
$\|w\|$. Since such a hyperplane need not exist, each sample is given a slack
$\xi_i\ge0$ by which it may fall short of its own margin, penalised linearly with
weight $C>0$. This gives the soft-margin ($C$-)SVM:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:primal}
\tag{P}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{w\in\Hs,\,b\in\R,\,\xi\in\R^m}\quad & \tfrac12\|w\|^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^m \xi_i\\
\text{s.t.}\quad & y_i\bigl(\inner{w}{\phi_i}+b\bigr)\ \ge\ 1-\xi_i, \qquad i=1,\dots,m,\\
& \xi_i\ \ge\ 0, \qquad i=1,\dots,m .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\|w\|$ measures model complexity and $C$ weighs the empirical loss against
generalisation. Problem~\eqref{eq:primal} is a convex QP, strictly convex in $w$ but
only convex in $(b,\xi)$.

Eliminating $\xi$ gives the equivalent unconstrained, non-smooth form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hinge}
\min_{w,b}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^m\bigl[1-y_i(\inner{w}{\phi_i}+b)\bigr]^+ ,
\qquad [t]^+:=\max\{0,t\},
\end{equation}
which is the form in which the SVM appears in the course material \cite{CMslides} as
the motivating example for nondifferentiable optimization. We shall meet it again, unexpectedly, in
Section~\ref{sec:whatitis}.

\section{Dual formulation}
Attach $\alpha_i\ge0$ to the margin constraints and $\mu_i\ge0$ to $\xi_i\ge0$:
\[
L(w,b,\xi;\alpha,\mu)=\tfrac12\|w\|^2+C\one^{\!\top}\xi
+\sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_i\bigl(1-\xi_i-y_i(\inner{w}{\phi_i}+b)\bigr)-\mu^{\!\top}\xi .
\]
Stationarity in $(w,b,\xi)$ gives the three conditions
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:representer}
w=\sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i y_i \phi_i,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i y_i = 0,
\qquad
\alpha_i+\mu_i=C .
\end{equation}
The first of these deserves a name and a moment's attention, because
Chapter~\ref{ch:dual} dualises precisely it. It says that at the optimum $w$ is a
linear combination of the training points, with coefficients $\alpha_iy_i$. This is
the \emph{representer identity}, the same statement as the representer theorem of
kernel methods, and it is what makes kernels usable at all: however large the
dimension of $\Hs$, the optimal $w$ is described by $m$ numbers.

It is also where $Q$ comes from. Substituting it into $\tfrac12\|w\|^2$ turns a
squared norm into
$\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\kappa(x_i,x_j)=\tfrac12\alpha^{\!\top}Q\alpha$.
Before the substitution there is no $Q$ anywhere in the problem. After it there is a
dense $m\times m$ matrix. So the dense Hessian of~\eqref{eq:dual} is not intrinsic to
the SVM. It is the price of eliminating $w$.

Substituting the three conditions back into $L$ makes every term collapse. The
quadratic terms combine into $-\tfrac12\alpha^{\!\top}Q\alpha$ with
$K_{ij}=\kappa(x_i,x_j)$, $Y=\operatorname{diag}(y)$ and $Q=YKY$, while the $b$ and
$\xi$ terms vanish by the second and third conditions, leaving $\one^{\!\top}\alpha-\tfrac12\alpha^{\!\top}Q\alpha$ to be maximised. Two
remarks on how the constraints of the dual arise, because both are easy to state
loosely:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mu_i\ge0$ and $\mu_i=C-\alpha_i$ together give $\alpha_i\le C$, which with
      $\alpha_i\ge0$ is the box $0\le\alpha\le C\one$;
\item $b$ enters $L$ only through the term $-b\sum_i\alpha_iy_i$, and $b$ is
      \emph{free}, so $\inf_b L=-\infty$ unless $\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0$. The
      $b$-stationarity condition therefore does not disappear when it is used to
      cancel that term: it survives as the constraint $y^{\!\top}\alpha=0$.
\end{itemize}
Writing the result as a minimisation of the negative, the dual of~\eqref{eq:primal}
is the convex QP
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dual}
\tag{D}
\min_{\alpha\in\R^m}\ \ \tfrac12\,\alpha^{\!\top} Q\,\alpha-\one^{\!\top}\alpha
\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad \alpha\in D:=\bigl\{\alpha:\ y^{\!\top}\alpha=0,\ \ 0\le\alpha\le C\one\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
$D$ is a non-empty ($0\in D$) compact polytope and $Q\succeq0$, so~\eqref{eq:dual}
always has a solution. Slater's condition holds for~\eqref{eq:primal}, since taking $\xi$ large enough gives
a strictly feasible point. There is therefore no duality gap: primal and dual optima
coincide and the KKT conditions
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kkt}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_i=0\quad &\Longrightarrow\quad y_if(x_i)\ge1,\\
0<\alpha_i<C\quad &\Longrightarrow\quad y_if(x_i)=1,\\
\alpha_i=C\quad &\Longrightarrow\quad y_if(x_i)\le1,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
characterise optimality, where $f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\kappa(x_i,x)+b$. Samples with $\alpha_i>0$ are the \emph{support vectors}, and those with
$0<\alpha_i<C$ are the \emph{free} support vectors. We will see in
Section~\ref{sec:cost} that their number controls the cost of (A1).

\section{Primal and dual variables}
\label{sec:recovery}
Given an optimal $\alpha^\star$ of~\eqref{eq:dual}, the primal variables come back as
\[
w^\star=\sum_i\alpha^\star_iy_i\phi_i,
\qquad
\xi^\star_i=\bigl[\,1-y_i(\inner{w^\star}{\phi_i}+b^\star)\,\bigr]^+ ,
\]
The first follows directly from the representer identity. The second follows from
primal feasibility of the margin constraint together with complementarity on
$\xi_i\ge0$. Note the factor $y_i$ inside: $\xi_i$ measures the violation of
\emph{the sample's own} margin, not of the hyperplane equation.

The bias $b^\star$ needs more care. The textbook recipe reads it off any free support
vector via~\eqref{eq:kkt}, averaging over them for stability. But the average is only
as good as the set of indices identified as free, so a solver that is close to
optimal rather than at it produces a $b$ that spoils the measured primal--dual
gap. The better observation is that $b$ enters the Lagrangian only through
$-b\sum_i\alpha_iy_i$, so \emph{$b$ is the multiplier of the equality constraint
$y^{\!\top}\alpha=0$}. Any algorithm that produces that multiplier explicitly can use
it directly, with no support-vector bookkeeping at all. For an interior-point method
it is one of the iterates. In our case the knapsack of Section~\ref{sec:knapsack}
supplies it at every oracle call, as~\eqref{eq:theta_is_b} proves by LP duality.

\section{Kernel trick}
Only inner products appear in~\eqref{eq:dual}, so $\phi$ need never be formed. The
kernels used in this report are
\begin{itemize}
\item linear: $\kappa(x,x')=x^{\!\top}x'$;
\item polynomial: $\kappa(x,x')=(\gamma\,x^{\!\top}x'+c_0)^d$, $\gamma>0$;
\item Gaussian (RBF): $\kappa(x,x')=\exp(-\gamma\|x-x'\|^2)$, $\gamma>0$.
\end{itemize}
We will use all three. The linear kernel is the case in which the dual variable of
(A1) stays in $\R^n$ and no Gram matrix is needed, and the other two exercise the
kernelised form of Section~\ref{sec:kernelised}, where it lives in $\R^m$.
For a nonlinear kernel $\Hs$ may be infinite-dimensional, unlike the linear case.
Section~\ref{sec:kernelised} explains how we represent the dual variable of (A1) in
that case.

\chapter{Multiclass SVM}
\label{ch:multiclass}

The SVM is a binary classifier. With $q$ classes and labels
$c_i\in\{1,\dots,q\}$, One-vs-Rest solves $q$ independent binary problems: for class
$k$ set
\[
y^k_i=\begin{cases}+1,&c_i=k\\ -1,&c_i\neq k\end{cases}
\]
and solve~\eqref{eq:primal}/\eqref{eq:dual} with $y=y^k$, obtaining
$f^k(x)=\sum_i\alpha^k_iy^k_i\kappa(x_i,x)+b^k$. A new point is assigned to
$\argmax_k f^k(x)$. It constructs $q$ models, against $q(q-1)/2$ for One-vs-One. See \cite{HsuLin02} for
a comparison of the two.

Note that three observations matter for what follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The $q$ problems share the same kernel matrix $K$. They differ only through
      the label vector, since $Q^k=Y^kKY^k$ and $D^k$ depends on $k$ only
      through the single equality $(y^k)^{\!\top}\alpha=0$. $Q^k$ is never formed.
\item The classifiers are trained independently, but at prediction time we compare them
      on a common scale that nothing in the training enforces. This produces the
      familiar One-vs-Rest pathologies: ties ($f^k(x)>0$ for two $k$) and rejections
      ($f^k(x)<0$ for all $k$).
\item Each binary problem is unbalanced, roughly $1:(q-1)$.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{The Lagrangian Dual}
\label{ch:dual}

The goal of this chapter is to choose which constraints to dualise, and to derive
the dual function that the bundle method of Chapter~\ref{ch:bundle} will maximise.
We follow the treatment of partial Lagrangian relaxation in the course material on
constrained optimization \cite{CMslides}, to which the project statement refers.
We first recall what a Lagrangian relaxation gives us, then show that the obvious
candidates in the SVM dual are the wrong ones, and finally dualise the constraint
that works.

\section{What a Lagrangian relaxation is for}
\label{sec:whyDual}
We recall only what the choice has to buy, in the notation of the course material
\cite{CMslides}. Relaxing $Ax\le b$ in $\min\{f(x):Ax\le b,\ x\in X\}$ with
multipliers $\lambda$ leaves the \emph{relaxed problem}
$\psi(\lambda)=\min\{f(x)+\lambda(b-Ax):x\in X\}$, one for each $\lambda$; solving it
once is a call to the \emph{oracle}, which returns $\psi(\lambda)$ and the
supergradient $b-Ax(\lambda)$. Whatever $f$ and $X$ are, $\psi$ is concave and
$\psi(\lambda)\le v(\mathrm{P})$, so every call gives a valid bound. The price is a
search over $\lambda$, so the choice turns on what is left in the relaxed problem:
the constraints to dualise are the complicating ones, and the ones to keep are those
that make the remainder easy.

\section{Which constraints to dualise}
\label{sec:choice}
For a Lagrangian dual approach to be worth building, the relaxation must satisfy
three requirements:
\begin{description}
\item[(R1)] the relaxed problem must be \emph{substantially easier} than the
  original, ideally closed-form or combinatorial;
\item[(R2)] the dual must be \emph{multi-dimensional}, otherwise a one-dimensional
  search solves it and bundle machinery is pointless;
\item[(R3)] the dual should be \emph{genuinely non-differentiable}, otherwise a
  smooth method is preferable.
\end{description}

The obvious candidate is the single equality $y^{\!\top}\alpha=0$. Attaching to it a
free multiplier $\theta\in\R$ and keeping the box as the easy constraint gives the
dual function
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:badrelax}
\psi(\theta)=\min\Bigl\{\tfrac12\alpha^{\!\top}Q\alpha-\one^{\!\top}\alpha
+\theta\,y^{\!\top}\alpha \ :\ 0\le\alpha\le C\one\Bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Since $y^{\!\top}\alpha=0$ is what remained of stationarity in $b$, dropping it
removes the bias from the model: \eqref{eq:badrelax} is the bias-free SVM with a
linear term $\theta\,y^{\!\top}\alpha$ pricing the class imbalance, and choosing
$\theta$ amounts to choosing how hard to push the solution towards balance. It is a
natural thing to try. It fails all three tests at once:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{(R1)} the minimisation in~\eqref{eq:badrelax} is still a QP with the
      \emph{same dense Hessian} $Q$. A single scalar constraint has been removed and
      the whole difficulty left in place.
\item \textbf{(R2)} $\theta$ is one number, so the Lagrangian dual is univariate and
      a bisection solves it in a handful of evaluations, leaving nothing for a
      bundle method to do.
\item \textbf{(R3)} worse still, when $Q\succ0$, the typical case for a Gaussian
      kernel, the minimiser of~\eqref{eq:badrelax} is unique. Then $\psi\in C^1$ and
      the dual is smooth, so even a nonsmooth method would be the wrong tool.
\end{itemize}
Relaxing the box $\alpha\le C\one$ instead fares no better: the relaxation is again a
QP in $Q$, and now with $m$ multipliers but no easier a subproblem.

The reason is structural. In~\eqref{eq:dual} no constraint is the difficulty. The
difficulty is $Q$ itself, a dense $m\times m$ matrix. So the object to dualise is not
a constraint of~\eqref{eq:dual} at all. It is the identity that created $Q$.

\section[Putting $w$ back]{Putting $w$ back: dualising the representer identity}
$Q$ appears in~\eqref{eq:dual} only because $w$ was eliminated through the
representer identity~\eqref{eq:representer}. In the standard derivation that identity
is used as a \emph{substitution}: a fact about the optimum, exploited to remove a
variable. A substitution cannot be dualised, but a constraint can. So we put $w$
back as an independent variable and \emph{impose} the identity instead of using it:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:split}
\tag{D$'$}
\min_{\alpha,\,w}\ \ \tfrac12\|w\|^2-\one^{\!\top}\alpha
\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad
w=\sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_iy_i\phi_i,
\qquad \alpha\in D .
\end{equation}
Substituting the constraint recovers~\eqref{eq:dual} exactly, so~\eqref{eq:split}
and~\eqref{eq:dual} are the same problem. Now dualise \emph{only} the linking
identity, with a free multiplier $\lambda\in\Hs$:
\[
L(\alpha,w;\lambda)=\tfrac12\|w\|^2-\one^{\!\top}\alpha
+\Bigl\langle \lambda,\ w-\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\phi_i \Bigr\rangle .
\]
The point of doing this is that $w$ and $\alpha$ are now \emph{untied}. They are two
descriptions of the same hyperplane, and~\eqref{eq:split} forces them to agree.
Relaxing that constraint lets them disagree, at a price $\lambda$, so each can be
optimised in whichever form suits it. The two groups of variables then separate. Minimising over $w\in\Hs$ is unconstrained
and gives $w(\lambda)=-\lambda$ with value $-\tfrac12\|\lambda\|^2$. What remains is
\emph{linear} in $\alpha$ over $D$, because $\alpha$ now appears only linearly. The
matrix $Q$ never forms: it existed only because $w$ and $\alpha$ were the same
object, and they no longer are. Hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:psi}
\boxed{\ \
\psi(\lambda)\ =\ -\tfrac12\|\lambda\|^2
\ +\ \min_{\alpha\in D}\ c(\lambda)^{\!\top}\alpha,
\qquad
c_i(\lambda)=-\bigl(1+y_i\inner{\lambda}{\phi_i}\bigr),
\ \ }
\end{equation}
and the Lagrangian dual of~\eqref{eq:split} is $\max_{\lambda\in\Hs}\psi(\lambda)$.

In the vocabulary of partial Lagrangian relaxation, the representer identity is the
\emph{complicating} constraint, since it couples $w$ to $\alpha$ and so creates the
dense $Q$. The constraints $\alpha\in D$ are the \emph{easy} ones, kept in the relaxed
problem because they are what makes it a knapsack. The usual warning that
$\psi(\lambda)$ may be $-\infty$ for some $\lambda$, so that the offending directions
of zero curvature have to be neutralised, does not apply here. $D$ is compact, so the
relaxed problem always has a finite optimum and $\psi$ is finite on all of $\Hs$.

\section{The relaxed problem is a continuous knapsack}
\label{sec:knapsack}
Of the two pieces the relaxed problem splits into, the one in $w$ was disposed of in
closed form above. The one that is actually solved at every oracle call is the piece
in $\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kp}
\tag{KP}
\min\ c^{\!\top}\alpha \qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad y^{\!\top}\alpha=0,\quad 0\le\alpha\le C\one,
\end{equation}
a linear objective, box bounds, and \emph{exactly one} further linear constraint.
That is the defining shape of the classical \emph{continuous knapsack} problem. It is
called continuous, or fractional, because each $\alpha_i$ may take any value in
$[0,C]$, where the $0/1$ knapsack has to take an item whole. The name matters because
such problems can be solved exactly by sorting, with no linear programming solver
involved. Relaxing that one equality with a scalar $\theta$
makes the box minimiser explicit,
\[
\alpha_i(\theta)=\begin{cases}
C, & c_i-\theta y_i<0,\\
0, & c_i-\theta y_i>0,\\
\text{any value in }[0,C], & c_i-\theta y_i=0 ,
\end{cases}
\]
and $h(\theta):=y^{\!\top}\alpha(\theta)$ is non-decreasing, so a $\theta^\star$ with
$h(\theta^\star)=0$ exists and is found by sorting the $m$ breakpoints $y_ic_i$ and
scanning (or by median search in $O(m)$ expected time). Splitting the tied components
to make the equality hold exactly produces an $\alpha$ that minimises the Lagrangian
of~\eqref{eq:kp} \emph{and} is feasible, hence optimal. Forming $c$ costs one kernel
product, and the rest costs $O(m\log m)$. This settles (R1): a dense QP has been replaced
by a sort. The scalar $\theta$ introduced here to solve~\eqref{eq:kp} is not just a
device. Section~\ref{sec:whatitis} shows that it is the SVM bias $b$.

The same structure appears in the course material: relax a single equality, and the
problem becomes separable, with a solution piecewise linear in the multiplier and one
breakpoint per variable. It appears there as a worked exercise in the course material \cite{CMslides} for the
projection onto a hyperplane, and (KP) is its bounded-variable analogue.

\section{Properties of the dual function}
\label{sec:psiprops}
\begin{proposition}[finiteness]
Note first that $D$ is a non-empty compact polytope, so the minimum
in~\eqref{eq:psi} is attained and finite for every $\lambda$. Hence $\psi:\Hs\to\R$ is finite everywhere and concave
(and upper semicontinuous, so that it does not jump upwards in the limit, which is
the regularity a maximisation needs for its optimum to be attained).
\end{proposition}
This is convenient. No feasibility (``infinite'') cuts are ever needed, and every
oracle call returns a valid lower bound on the optimal value.

\begin{proposition}[structure and non-differentiability]
Write
\[
\psi=\psi_{\mathrm{sm}}+\psi_{\mathrm{pl}},
\qquad
\psi_{\mathrm{sm}}(\lambda)=-\tfrac12\|\lambda\|^2,
\quad
\psi_{\mathrm{pl}}(\lambda)=\min_{\alpha\in D}c(\lambda)^{\!\top}\alpha .
\]
Then $\psi_{\mathrm{sm}}$ is smooth and known in closed form, and
$\psi_{\mathrm{pl}}$ is a minimum of finitely many affine functions of $\lambda$ (one
per vertex of $D$), hence concave \emph{polyhedral}: piecewise linear, with finitely
many pieces. $\psi$ fails to be differentiable exactly where
\eqref{eq:kp} has multiple optima, i.e.\ where some reduced cost vanishes,
$c_i-\theta^\star y_i=0$.
\end{proposition}
Unwinding that condition with $w=-\lambda$ gives
$y_i\inner{w}{\phi_i}+\theta^{\star}y_i=1$. So the kinks of $\psi$ sit on the
support-vector condition $y_if(x_i)=1$ of~\eqref{eq:kkt}. Any non-degenerate SVM has
free support vectors, so the maximiser of $\psi$ is itself a point of
non-differentiability. This settles (R3). The non-smoothness is not incidental: it is
where the solution lives.

\begin{proposition}[no duality gap]
\eqref{eq:split} is convex, its coupling constraint is affine and $D$ is compact.
Therefore $\max_\lambda\psi(\lambda)=v(\mathrm{D}')=v(\mathrm{D})$, and the maximiser
is $\lambda^\star=-w^\star$.
\end{proposition}
So the multiplier of the relaxed identity is, up to sign, the primal weight vector
itself, and the bundle method's task is to find the price at which $w$ and $\alpha$
agree again. A supergradient of $\psi$ at $\lambda$ is the violation of the relaxed
constraint,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:supergrad}
g(\lambda)=w(\lambda)-\sum_i\alpha_i(\lambda)y_i\phi_i=-\lambda-\sum_i\alpha_i(\lambda)y_i\phi_i ,
\end{equation}
available at no extra cost from the oracle. (R2) holds as well: $\lambda$ ranges over
$\Hs$, of dimension $n$ for the linear kernel and $m$ after the reduction of
Section~\ref{sec:kernelised} in general.

\section{What this dual actually is}
\label{sec:whatitis}
It is worth identifying $\max_\lambda\psi$ in closed form. Substituting $w=-\lambda$
in~\eqref{eq:psi},
\[
\max_{\lambda}\psi(\lambda)
\iff
-\min_{w}\Bigl\{\tfrac12\|w\|^2+\max_{\alpha\in D}\sum_i\alpha_i\bigl(1-y_i\inner{w}{\phi_i}\bigr)\Bigr\},
\]
and dualising the single equality inside the inner maximisation with a scalar
$\theta$ gives, by LP duality,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:theta_is_b}
\max_{\alpha\in D}\sum_i\alpha_i\bigl(1-y_ig_i\bigr)
=\min_{\theta}\ C\sum_i\bigl[1-y_i(g_i+\theta)\bigr]^+ ,
\qquad g_i:=\inner{w}{\phi_i} .
\end{equation}
So the Lagrangian dual of~\eqref{eq:split} is the hinge-loss
primal~\eqref{eq:hinge}, and \emph{the multiplier $\theta$ of the knapsack equality
is the SVM bias $b$}, so $b$ falls out of the oracle for free.

That the dual of the dual returns the primal is not an accident of this problem. It
is the general fact that taking the conjugate of a closed convex function twice
recovers it. The next section says what is nevertheless gained.

\section{Relation to the direct nonsmooth formulation}
\label{sec:bmrm}
The SVM appears in the course material on nondifferentiable unconstrained
optimization \cite{CMslides} as the motivating example, in the form
\[
\min_{w,b}\ \ \|w\|^2+C\sum_{i\in I}\max\bigl\{1-y_i(\inner{w}{x_i}-b),\,0\bigr\},
\]
whose nondifferentiability is precisely what motivates bundle methods. Since
Section~\ref{sec:whatitis} shows that $\max_\lambda\psi$ is that same function, we
must say clearly what dualising the representer identity adds over simply running a
bundle method on it, which is what the published cutting-plane methods for
regularised risk minimisation do \cite{Joachims06,Teo10,Franc08}.

It is not that the bias makes a direct approach impossible. $f(w,b)$ is convex in
$(w,b)$ jointly and a subgradient in both variables is immediate, so $b$ can simply
go in the bundle. The gain is that our relaxation minimises over $b$ \emph{exactly}
at every oracle call, instead of approximating the $b$ direction with cutting
planes. The knapsack multiplier $\theta$
of~\eqref{eq:theta_is_b} is that partial minimisation, and the sort of
Section~\ref{sec:knapsack} is how it is computed. Put another way, we bundle
$g(w)=\min_b f(w,b)$ rather than $f(w,b)$, and $g$ is the better-conditioned object,
one of its directions having been eliminated in closed form.

We expect this to matter in practice. A cutting-plane model has to spend cuts on
every direction it approximates, and the $b$ direction is one that we can remove
exactly for the price of a sort. Eliminating it should therefore buy a lower
iteration count, and comparing (A1) against a bundle method applied directly to
$f(w,b)$, with everything else held equal, is the experiment that would settle it.
We intend to report that comparison.

We claim exactly this much: the family of cuts and the function being optimised are
the same as in the direct approach, and what relaxing the representer identity adds
is (i) the exact elimination of $b$ at every oracle call, (ii) the SVM dual variables
through the aggregated solution of Section~\ref{sec:dw}, and (iii) a two-sided bound
on $v(\mathrm{D})$ rather than a bound on one's own objective only.

\chapter{The Bundle Method}
\label{ch:bundle}

The goal of this chapter is to describe the algorithm we use to maximise $\psi$, and
to show what the master problem gives us besides the optimal multiplier. We first
recall the cutting-plane model and its stabilised master, then specialise both to
our $\psi$, and finally read off the primal solution.

\section{The cutting-plane model and the bundle}
\label{sec:cpmodel}
We recall the method only far enough to fix notation, following the course material
\cite{CMslides} and \cite{Frangioni20}; our setting is concave maximisation, so every
inequality below is reversed with respect to the usual convex statement.

The oracle at $\lambda$ returns $\psi(\lambda)$ and a supergradient $g(\lambda)$.
Since $\psi$ is concave, one call bounds it \emph{everywhere},
$\psi(\mu)\le\ell_\lambda(\mu):=\psi(\lambda)+\inner{g(\lambda)}{\mu-\lambda}$ for all
$\mu$, and this global validity is what makes first-order information useful here.
The collected triples form the \emph{bundle} $\mathcal B=\{(\lambda^h,\psi^h,g^h)\}$,
whose pointwise minimum $\psi_{\mathcal B}\ge\psi$ is the \emph{cutting-plane model};
maximising it is the \emph{master problem}. Because the bare model over-estimates
where few cuts sit, the master is stabilised around a \emph{stability centre}
$\bar\lambda$ by a proximal term with parameter $t$, and a \emph{serious step} moves
the centre when the true $\psi$ delivers a fixed fraction $\kappa$ of the predicted
improvement, a \emph{null step} keeping it and the new cut otherwise. The course
material writes the stabilisation with $\mu=1/t$ multiplying rather than dividing.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig-cuttingplane.pdf}
\caption{The horizontal axis is $\mu$, the free variable of the inequality above.
Here $\lambda^1$ and $\lambda^2$ are the two points at which the oracle has been called,
and the drawing is the one-dimensional case, so that the supporting hyperplanes
appear as lines. One oracle call at $\lambda$ returns $\psi(\lambda)$ and a

hyperplanes collected so far, so it too lies above $\psi$, and its maximum is an
upper bound on $\psi^\star$. Each new call adds a hyperplane, which can only lower
the minimum. The method probes next where the model is highest, since that is the
only place the maximum can be.}
\label{fig:cp}
\end{figure}

\section{The structured master problem}
\label{sec:master}
\label{sec:master}
We depart from the generic scheme of Section~\ref{sec:cpmodel} in one respect.
Writing $\psi=\psi_{\mathrm{sm}}+\psi_{\mathrm{pl}}$ as in Chapter~\ref{ch:dual},
the smooth part $\psi_{\mathrm{sm}}(\lambda)=-\tfrac12\|\lambda\|^2$ is known in
closed form. Approximating it with cuts gains nothing and loses something, because a
piecewise-linear model of a curved function is poor near its maximiser. We therefore
linearise only $\psi_{\mathrm{pl}}$ and carry $\psi_{\mathrm{sm}}$ into the master
unchanged. This is a \emph{generalized}, or structured, bundle method. Each oracle call
returns some $\alpha^j\in D$, which gives the affine upper model
\[
\psi_{\mathrm{pl}}(\lambda)\ \le\ c(\lambda)^{\!\top}\alpha^j
= -\,\one^{\!\top}\alpha^j-\inner{\lambda}{\textstyle\sum_i\alpha^j_iy_i\phi_i}
=: -a_j-\inner{\lambda}{b_j} .
\]
With a stability centre $\bar\lambda$ and proximal parameter $t>0$ the master is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:master}
\max_{\lambda\in\Hs}\ \
-\tfrac12\|\lambda\|^2
+\min_{j=1,\dots,J}\bigl\{-a_j-\inner{\lambda}{b_j}\bigr\}
-\tfrac{1}{2t}\|\lambda-\bar\lambda\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Exchanging $\min$ and $\max$ and eliminating $\lambda$ gives its dual, a QP over the
unit simplex $\Delta^J$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:masterdual}
\min_{\vartheta\in\Delta^J}\ \
\frac{1}{2\rho}\Bigl\|\tfrac{\bar\lambda}{t}-\textstyle\sum_j\vartheta_jb_j\Bigr\|^2
-\ \textstyle\sum_j\vartheta_j a_j ,
\qquad \rho:=1+\tfrac1t,
\end{equation}
after which
$\lambda^{\mathrm{new}}=\bigl(\bar\lambda/t-\sum_j\vartheta_jb_j\bigr)/\rho$. Only
the $J\times J$ Gram matrix $G_{jl}=\inner{b_j}{b_l}$ and the vector
$p_j=\inner{b_j}{\bar\lambda}$ are needed, and we maintain both incrementally, so a
master costs $O(J^2)$ plus one kernel product per new cut. We solve it with an
off-the-shelf QP solver, which the project statement allows.

Our variant differs from the generic scheme in two respects. The smooth part is
carried in the master exactly rather than cut, as above, and we stop on the
\emph{certified gap} of Section~\ref{sec:dw}, which brackets $v(\mathrm{D})$ from
both sides at no extra cost, rather than on the usual test on the length of the
step.

\section{The variant we use}
\label{sec:variant}
Three choices complete the description of the algorithm.

\emph{Initialisation.} We start from $\lambda=0$, which is admissible because $\psi$
is finite everywhere. The first oracle call then minimises $-\one^{\!\top}\alpha$
over $D$, so the first cut is generated by a vertex of $D$ at which as much mass as
the equality permits sits at the upper bound, and $\psi(0)$ is already a valid lower
bound on $v(\mathrm{D})$.

\emph{Keeping the bundle bounded.} Left alone the bundle grows by one cut per
iteration, and the master grows with it. We cap the number of cuts. When the cap is
reached the oldest cuts are replaced by the single \emph{aggregate} cut generated by
$\hat\alpha=\sum_j\vartheta_j\alpha^j$, which is legitimate because
$\hat\alpha\in D$, so the aggregate is as valid a cut as any the oracle returns.
Since Section~\ref{sec:cost} argues that the number of cuts needed tracks the number
of free support vectors, the cap has to be set above that number, and we will report
what happens when it is not.

\emph{The serious-step test.} Writing $\psi_{\mathcal B}$ for the model of
Section~\ref{sec:master}, that is the exact quadratic plus the cuts, and
$\lambda^{\mathrm{new}}$ for the master's solution, we take a serious step when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sstest}
\psi(\lambda^{\mathrm{new}})-\psi(\bar\lambda)\ \ge\
\kappa\,\bigl[\psi_{\mathcal B}(\lambda^{\mathrm{new}})-\psi(\bar\lambda)\bigr],
\qquad \kappa\in(0,1),
\end{equation}
and a null step otherwise. Since $\psi_{\mathcal B}\ge\psi$ everywhere, the bracket is
the improvement the model predicted and is non-negative, so the test asks that the
true function deliver at least the fraction $\kappa$ of what was promised.

\emph{Parameters.} The variant has four: the proximal parameter $t$, the fraction
$\kappa$ of~\eqref{eq:sstest}, the cap on the bundle, and the tolerance on the
certified gap, the run stopping when the bracket of Section~\ref{sec:dw} falls below
it. We increase $t$ after a serious step and decrease it after a null step, which is
the usual heuristic. Their effect is one of the things the
experiments have to establish, since none of them has a value that theory fixes.

\section{Primal recovery and Dantzig--Wolfe}
\label{sec:dw}
Let $\hat\alpha:=\sum_j\vartheta_j\alpha^j$ be the \emph{aggregated solution} built
from the master problem's dual multipliers. Since $\vartheta\in\Delta^J$ and $D$ is
convex, $\hat\alpha\in D$: it is a \emph{feasible} solution of~\eqref{eq:dual} at
every iteration. Moreover $\sum_j\vartheta_jb_j=\Phi^{\!\top}Y\hat\alpha$ and
$\sum_j\vartheta_ja_j=\one^{\!\top}\hat\alpha$, so for $t\to\infty$
\eqref{eq:masterdual} becomes
\[
\min_{\vartheta\in\Delta^J}\ \tfrac12\,\hat\alpha^{\!\top}Q\hat\alpha-\one^{\!\top}\hat\alpha ,
\]
i.e.\ \emph{the SVM dual~\eqref{eq:dual} restricted to
$\mathrm{conv}\{\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^J\}\subseteq D$}. The method is therefore exactly \emph{Dantzig--Wolfe decomposition} \cite{DW60}
of~\eqref{eq:dual}: the scheme that optimises over a polytope by keeping an inner
approximation of it, here $\mathrm{conv}\{\alpha^j\}$, and enlarging that
approximation with points supplied by a cheap subproblem, called the \emph{pricing
problem}, which here is~\eqref{eq:kp}. Generating those points one at a time is
\emph{column generation}. Bundle on $\psi$, cutting planes on~\eqref{eq:hinge} and
column generation on~\eqref{eq:dual} are thus three views of one algorithm. Two practical consequences:
\begin{itemize}
\item the master returns the primal solution $\hat\alpha\to\alpha^\star$, so we need
      nothing extra to recover the classifier,
\item $\psi(\lambda)\le v(\mathrm{D})\le
      \tfrac12\hat\alpha^{\!\top}Q\hat\alpha-\one^{\!\top}\hat\alpha$ at \emph{every}
      iteration, a \textbf{certified gap}, which decomposition algorithms for the
      SVM such as SMO do not provide.
\end{itemize}

\section{Kernelised form}
\label{sec:kernelised}
For a general kernel $\Hs$ may be infinite-dimensional, so write
$\lambda=\sum_j\beta_j\phi_j$. This is without loss of generality: a component of
$\lambda$ orthogonal to $\mathrm{span}\{\phi_i\}$ leaves $c(\lambda)$ unchanged and
only increases $\|\lambda\|^2$, so at the optimum it is zero. Then
$\inner{\lambda}{\phi_i}=(K\beta)_i$ and $\|\lambda\|^2=\beta^{\!\top}K\beta$, and one
oracle call costs one matrix--vector product with $K$ plus a sort. Note that the
correct stabilising term is the \emph{RKHS} norm
$\|\lambda-\bar\lambda\|^2=(\beta-\bar\beta)^{\!\top}K(\beta-\bar\beta)$ rather than
the Euclidean $\|\beta-\bar\beta\|^2$. It is the $K$-metric that
makes~\eqref{eq:masterdual} come out in the form above.

\section{Cost, and how many cuts are needed}
\label{sec:cost}
Per iteration: one oracle call ($O(m^2)$ for the kernel product, $O(m\log m)$ for the
sort) plus one master ($O(J^2)$ in the QP, $O(Jm)$ to update $G$). The interesting
question is how large $J$ must grow. The oracle returns essentially \emph{vertices}
of $D$, that is $0/C$ vectors, and $\hat\alpha$ is a convex combination of them: a
component of $\alpha^\star$ sitting at a bound is produced by a single vertex, but
every \emph{free} support vector $0<\alpha^\star_i<C$ must be produced by mixing
vertices that disagree on $i$. We therefore expect the number of cuts the method needs to be governed by the number
of free support vectors, and truncating the bundle below that number to make the gap
stall, which is the classical Dantzig--Wolfe tailing-off. If that is right, the
bundle has to be sized above the expected number of free support vectors, which
large $C$ and simple kernels keep small, and the count of free support vectors
becomes the quantity that predicts the cost of a run. Measuring this is part of the
experimental work to come.

\section{Does solving one One-vs-Rest problem help the others?}
\label{sec:transfer}
The project statement asks this explicitly. The $q$ problems differ only through
$y^k$, so there are three candidate mechanisms. They are not equally useful, and we
take them in turn.

\paragraph{The kernel matrix.} $K$ is computed once and shared. We never form $Q^k=Y^kKY^k$, because the oracle only ever needs $K\beta$. Better still, the $q$ oracles
can be run in lockstep so that their $q$ matrix--vector products become one BLAS-3
product $K\,[\beta^1\cdots\beta^q]$. This is a genuine, structural saving of a factor
$q$ in the dominant cost.

\paragraph{Warm-starting the multiplier.} Since $\lambda^\star=-w^\star$,
warm-starting class $k{+}1$ at class $k$'s optimum amounts to assuming the two
separating hyperplanes are similar. They are not: the positive class changes
completely between the two problems.

\paragraph{Transferring cuts.} Any point of $D^l$ generates a valid cut for class
$l$, and the $D^k$ differ only through the equality. Writing $P_k$, $P_l$ for the two
positive blocks and $R$ for the rest, $\alpha\in D^k$ satisfies
$\alpha(P_k)=\alpha(P_l)+\alpha(R)$, so $(y^l)^{\!\top}\alpha=-2\,\alpha(R)$: the
imbalance is known in closed form and can be repaired in $O(m)$ by moving mass onto
$P_l$, \emph{preserving the support pattern} of the transferred cut. This costs no
oracle call and no kernel product.

Only the first of the three is certain to pay. Sharing the kernel matrix saves a
factor $q$ in the dominant cost, and that saving is structural rather than
circumstantial. The other two are plausible but not obviously useful, and we are
cautious about both. Warm-starting the multiplier assumes that consecutive
hyperplanes are similar, which they need not be, since the positive class changes
completely from one problem to the next, and a stability centre inherited from the
wrong place costs iterations to undo. Transferring cuts does no harm, but a repaired
cut need not be an informative one. Which of the three is worth its complexity is a
question for the experiments.

\chapter{What to expect from the algorithms}
\label{ch:expect}

This chapter states the convergence and complexity properties we expect to see, the
assumptions they rest on, and whether our problem satisfies them.

\section{Assumptions, and whether we satisfy them}
The convergence theory of proximal bundle methods \cite{Frangioni20,HUL93} assumes a
convex (here concave) objective that is finite on the whole space, an oracle
returning its value and one subgradient, and bounded subgradients on the region
explored. We check these in turn.

\emph{Concavity} holds: $\psi$ is a minimum of affine functions of $\lambda$ plus the
concave term $-\tfrac12\|\lambda\|^2$ (Section~\ref{sec:psiprops}).

\emph{Finiteness} holds, and this is the assumption that most often fails in
Lagrangian relaxation. Because the relaxed problem is a minimisation over the compact
polytope $D$, its optimum is attained for every $\lambda$, so $\psi>-\infty$
everywhere. We therefore never need the feasibility cuts, nor the neutralisation of
zero-curvature directions, that the course material warns about for relaxations whose
inner problem can be unbounded.

\emph{Differentiability} does \emph{not} hold, and is not assumed. It fails exactly
where the knapsack has ties, which is the support-vector condition $y_if(x_i)=1$, and
the maximiser is such a point. This is why a bundle method is used rather than a
gradient method.

\emph{Bounded subgradients.} The supergradient is
$-\lambda-\Phi^{\!\top}Y\alpha(\lambda)$ with $\alpha(\lambda)\in D$ and
$\|\alpha\|_\infty\le C$, so on any bounded set of multipliers the supergradients are
bounded, and $\psi$ is Lipschitz there.

\emph{No duality gap.} Problem~\eqref{eq:split} is convex, its coupling constraint is
affine and $D$ is compact, so $\max_\lambda\psi=v(\mathrm{D})$. What the bundle method
converges to is therefore the value we want, not a bound on it.

\section{Convergence and complexity}
With these assumptions the proximal bundle method converges to the optimum, and the
complexity that the course material quotes for the nonsmooth convex case is
$O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations, the same order as the subgradient method, with the
practical difference lying in the constant and in the behaviour in the tail. We
expect the tail behaviour to matter here: the bundle accumulates cuts around the
maximiser, and the smooth part $-\tfrac12\|\lambda\|^2$ is carried in the master
exactly rather than approximated.

The strong concavity of that term is worth isolating, because it is what the
published analyses of cutting-plane methods for regularised risk minimisation
\cite{Teo10,Joachims06,Franc08} exploit to obtain $O(1/\varepsilon)$ rather than
$O(1/\varepsilon^2)$. Since Section~\ref{sec:whatitis} shows that our dual is that
same regularised risk, we expect their rate to apply to (A1) as well.

\emph{Per-iteration cost.} One oracle call is one kernel product, $O(m^2)$ in general
and $O(mn)$ for a linear kernel, plus an $O(m\log m)$ sort. One master problem is a
quadratic program over the unit simplex in $J$ variables, costing $O(J^2)$ in the
solver and $O(Jm)$ to update the Gram matrix incrementally. We expect $J$ to grow
with the number of free support vectors, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:cost}, so the dominant term should be $O(m^2)$ per iteration on
kernel problems.

For (A2) the interior-point solver on a dense convex quadratic program of order $m$
costs $O(m^3)$ per iteration in the factorisation, in a number of iterations that is
in practice nearly independent of $m$. This is the trade-off the experiments are
meant to expose: (A1) pays little per iteration and may need many, (A2) pays a great
deal per iteration and needs few.

\emph{A caveat on the citations.} We quote these results from the sources listed, and
before the final version we will verify each against the original and give the
theorem and page number, as the project guidelines require.

\chapter{The shared-slack-budget model}
\label{app:budget}

For completeness we record an alternative model that couples the One-vs-Rest
problems, together with a defect we found in it. Give each sample a single slack budget $B_i$ to be shared by all $q$ binary
classifiers:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:budget}
\tag{M-B}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{w,b,\xi}\quad & \sum_{k=1}^q\tfrac12\|w^k\|^2+C\sum_{i,k}\xi^k_i\\
\text{s.t.}\quad & y^k_i\bigl(\inner{w^k}{\phi_i}+b^k\bigr)\ \ge\ 1-\xi^k_i,
\qquad \xi^k_i\ \ge\ 0,
\qquad \sum_{k=1}^q \xi^k_i\ \le\ B_i .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Dualising only the $mq$ margin constraints with $\alpha^k_i\ge0$ separates the
variables. Minimising over $w^k$ gives the usual $w^k=\sum_i\alpha^k_iy^k_i\phi_i$
and $-\tfrac12(\alpha^k)^{\!\top}Q^k\alpha^k$, minimising over $b^k$ forces
$\sum_i\alpha^k_iy^k_i=0$, and minimising over $\xi$ splits across $i$ into a tiny LP
that is solved by inspection, putting the whole budget on the most negative
coefficient or using none of it. Hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:budgetdual}
\psi(\alpha)=\sum_{i,k}\alpha^k_i-\tfrac12\sum_{k}(\alpha^k)^{\!\top}Q^k\alpha^k
-\sum_{i}B_i\Bigl[\max_k\bigl(\alpha^k_i-C\bigr)\Bigr]^+ ,
\end{equation}
on $\{\alpha\ge0:\ \sum_i\alpha^k_iy^k_i=0\ \ \forall k\}$. The budget replaces the
box $\alpha\le C$ by a penalty, and the dual is non-differentiable where two or more
classes tie for a contested budget, which is where the One-vs-Rest coupling happens.

Writing this out showed us a defect. With a hard budget \eqref{eq:budget} can be
infeasible, and its Lagrangian dual is then unbounded. A sample lying where two
classifiers both score near zero needs $\xi^k_i\approx1$ from each, so $B_i<2$ is
usually infeasible already and one outlier destroys the training problem. The repair
is to soften the budget, $\sum_k\xi^k_i\le B_i+z_i$ with $z_i\ge0$ penalised by
$Dz_i$: the $\xi$ subproblem stays an LP, the model is always feasible, and
boundedness in $z$ restores the box $0\le\alpha\le(C+D)\one$.

We do not propose it as the main line. Even repaired it keeps the quadratic forms
$Q^k$ inside the dual function, so the bundle has to approximate a curved function
with planes, whereas the relaxation of Chapter~\ref{ch:dual} leaves an LP. And it
changes (M), so any comparison with One-vs-Rest would confound a modelling change
with an algorithmic one.
